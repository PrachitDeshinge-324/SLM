The question

A small model can label its own training data without an answer key: sample several answers and keep the ones most samples agree on. This page asks two things. First, does quantization spoil those labels? Second, when the model trains on them for several rounds, what does it actually learn?

    1. The idea: the vote as a teacher

    Draw $N$ answers to a question. Answers cut off at the token limit never vote. If enough of all $N$ samples agree, the majority answer $\hat a$ becomes the question's label, and the solutions that reach it become training data:

    $$\text{label } \hat a \quad\text{if}\quad \frac{n(\hat a)}{N} \ge \tau, \qquad \tau = 0.75 \;(\text{6 of 8})$$

    Two numbers decide whether this can work. Supply $n$ is how many questions get a label. Label accuracy $a$ is the share of those labels that are right, checked against the answer key only afterwards. A round of training therefore sees $n\,a$ right labels and $n(1-a)$ wrong ones. Self-training helps only if $a$ stays high as the loop goes on, because each round's trained model writes the next round's labels.

    The loop and the training objectives
    1. Round 0 is the untrained model. It answers the test questions (the control) and a pool of GSM8K train questions loaded without their answers.
    2. Pool questions are labelled by the rule above.
    3. A fresh LoRA adapter is trained on the frozen base at the same precision, every round.
    4. The base with the adapter applied but not merged (so a 4-bit base stays 4-bit) answers the same test questions with the same seeds, and answers the pool again for the next round's labels.

    SFT on voted solutions (main loop)

    $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\text{solutions}}\sum_{t}\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$$

    Next-token loss on every finished solution that reaches the voted answer; the prompt is masked. $T$ is the number of solution tokens in the batch.

    DPO on voted against minority (one round)

    $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\big[\Delta\bar\ell(\text{chosen}) - \Delta\bar\ell(\text{rejected})\big]\Big)$$

    One pair per question: a solution with the voted answer against one with another answer, of similar length. $\Delta\bar\ell$ is the mean per-token log-probability under the trained model minus under the frozen untrained model.

    2. Does quantization spoil the labels?

    • Fewer labels, about as accurate. Quantization mostly lowers how many questions reach the agreement threshold. Where a 4-bit model still agrees with itself, its label is usually about as often right as at 16-bit; Gemma is the exception. How much supply drops depends on the model.
    • The vote is the better label source. Asking the model to judge its own answers (the verifier) separates right from wrong voted answers worse than agreement does, and multiplying the two does not reliably help. Several models' verifiers give no usable verdicts at all.

    Each score below ranks questions by "the voted answer is right". AUROC is the chance a right voted answer outranks a wrong one (0.5 is no signal). Label accuracy at the vote's number of labels lets each score pick as many questions as the vote labels, so all three are compared at the same supply.

    3. Training on the vote at 16-bit and 4-bit

    3.1 What the gain is made of

    Single-answer accuracy $s$ is the product of finishing and being right when finished, where $c$ is the cut-off rate and $f$ the accuracy among finished answers:

    $$s = (1 - c)\,f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s_k - s_0 = \underbrace{(c_0 - c_k)\,f_0}_{\text{finishing more}} \;+\; \underbrace{(1 - c_k)\,(f_k - f_0)}_{\text{right more often when finished}}$$

    The split is exact. If self-training improved reasoning, the second term would carry the gain; if it only taught the model to stop in time, the first would. Each arm is compared with its own round 0.

    3.2 The labels drift

    Each round's model labels the pool again. If supply rises from $n_0$ to $n_k$ while accuracy moves from $a_0$ to $a_k$, the labels added since round 0 are right at the rate

    $$a_{\text{added}} = \frac{n_k a_k - n_0 a_0}{n_k - n_0}$$

    A low $a_{\text{added}}$ means the loop is growing its training set mostly at the margin, where the vote is least reliable.

    3.3 The vote sharpens around its own errors

    Apply the same label rule to the held-out test questions: a confident question is one where at least 6 of 8 samples agree, and the share of confident questions whose answer is right is the label accuracy the model would reach on questions it has never trained on.

    4. Does the 4-bit model catch up with 16-bit?

    Two quantities, both on the same test questions. The gap is how far round $k$ of the 4-bit model still trails the untrained 16-bit model; the difference of differences asks which arm gained more from its own training:

    $$\text{gap}_k = s^{16}_0 - s^{4}_k, \qquad \Delta^{16}_k - \Delta^{4}_k = (s^{16}_k - s^{16}_0) - (s^{4}_k - s^{4}_0)$$

    A gap that shrinks towards zero means training on its own votes brings the quantized model back to full-precision quality. A negative difference means the 4-bit model gained more.

    How these numbers are made
    • Every run is re-scored from its stored raw answers with the current code; nothing is read from metrics stored in the run files.
    • A cut-off answer counts as wrong in single-answer accuracy and never votes.
    • Within an experiment, every round is scored on the questions all its finished rounds share. A round or pool still being written is listed as in progress and left out until complete.
    • Label accuracy uses the train answers only after the run, to score labels the loop chose without them. No reference answer reaches label selection or training.
    • Intervals are 95% paired bootstrap intervals over questions.

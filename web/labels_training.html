About this page

The page has two parts.

  1. Part 1: the vote as a label source. Before a model can teach itself, it needs a way to tell which of its own answers to learn from, without an answer key. We measured two such signals at 16, 8 and 4-bit: agreement among 8 sampled answers (the vote), and the model judging its own answers (the verifier). This part is a self-contained measurement study.
  2. Part 2: learning from the vote. The model trains on its own voted answers for several rounds, at 16-bit and 4-bit. Fine-tuning on the voted solutions (SFT) is the main loop; preference training on voted against minority answers (DPO) was tested for one round. Runs still in progress appear as such and fill in when they sync.

1.1 How a question gets a label

For a question, draw $N$ answers. Cut-off answers (they hit the token limit) never vote. The majority answer $\hat a$ becomes the question's label when enough of all $N$ samples agree:

$$\text{label } \hat a \quad\text{if}\quad \frac{n(\hat a)}{N} \ge \tau, \qquad \tau = 0.75 \;(\text{6 of 8})$$

Labelled

The share of questions that receive a label. It is the supply of training data.

Label accuracy

The share of labels that equal the reference answer. It is computed after the fact; the rule itself never sees the answer key.

1.3 The vote against the verifier

Three label-free scores for "the voted answer is right": the vote's agreement, the verifier's approval of the solutions that carry the voted answer, and the two multiplied. Each is judged two ways.

  • AUROC: the chance that a question whose voted answer is right gets a higher score than one whose voted answer is wrong. 0.5 means no signal.
  • Label accuracy at the vote's number of labels: each score picks as many questions as the vote labels (agreement ≥ 0.75), and we check how many of those labels are right. All three are compared at the same amount of training data.

Rows are scored on the same questions as the Phase 2 page. A verifier is marked unusable when fewer than 90% of its verdicts can be parsed, or when it approves almost everything (97% or more) or almost nothing (5% or less); the summary uses only usable rows.

1.4 What Part 1 supports

  • Fewer labels, about as accurate. Quantization mostly lowers how many questions reach the agreement threshold. For most models, where a 4-bit model still agrees with itself its label is about as often right as at 16-bit; Gemma is the exception. Read the Labelled and Label accuracy columns of 1.2 together.
  • The vote is the label source. On its own, the model judging its own answers ranks right and wrong voted answers clearly worse than the vote does. Multiplying the two gives mixed results: it lowers AUROC in more rows than it raises it, and changes label accuracy at a fixed number of labels little (1.3). Verifiers of several models also fail to give usable verdicts at all.
  • How much quantization hurts depends on the model. Compare the change column across models in 1.2.
  • Calibration of agreement against accuracy at each precision is drawn on the Phase 1 page; false positive rates and MCC of the verifier are on the Phase 2 page.

As a paper on its own, this part is a measurement study: does weight quantization change how good a small model's self-labels are? Its limits: one quantizer (bitsandbytes), nothing below 4 bits, and mostly GSM8K.

2.1 The self-training loop

  1. Round 0 is the untrained model. It answers the test questions (the control) and a pool of GSM8K train questions loaded without their answers.
  2. Labels: pool questions are labelled by the rule in 1.1.
  3. Training: a fresh LoRA adapter on the frozen base, at the same precision, every round.
  4. Test: the base with the adapter applied but not merged, so a 4-bit base stays 4-bit, answers the same test questions with the same seeds.
  5. The trained model answers the pool again, and its votes become the next round's labels.

SFT on voted solutions

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\text{solutions}}\sum_{t}\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$$

Next-token loss on every finished solution that reaches the voted answer; the prompt is masked. $T$ is the number of solution tokens in the batch.

DPO on voted against minority

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\log\sigma\!\Big(\beta\big[\Delta\bar\ell(\text{chosen}) - \Delta\bar\ell(\text{rejected})\big]\Big)$$

One pair per question: a solution with the voted answer against one with another answer, of similar length. $\Delta\bar\ell$ is the mean per-token log-probability under the trained model minus under the frozen untrained model.

Every change in single-answer accuracy splits into finishing more often and being right more often when finished:

$$\text{single-answer accuracy} = (1 - \text{cut-off rate}) \times \text{right among finished}$$

Right among finished is pooled over questions, so it moves when questions drop out of it. The same questions column compares it question by question, on questions where both runs finished at least one answer.

How these numbers are made

  • Every run is re-scored from its stored raw answers with the current code; nothing is read from metrics stored in the run files.
  • A cut-off answer counts as wrong in single-answer accuracy and never votes.
  • Within an experiment, every round is scored on the questions all its finished rounds share. A round or pool that is still being written is listed as in progress and left out until it is complete.
  • Label accuracy uses the train answers only after the run, to score labels the loop chose without them.
  • Intervals are 95% paired bootstrap intervals over questions.

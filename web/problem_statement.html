The Core Problem & Datasets

Executive Summary

This project investigates a critical gap in on-device AI: Does quantization destroy a small model's ability to evaluate its own reasoning? We use GSM8K as our primary benchmark because it provides the perfect difficulty curve (~60% base accuracy) to measure verifier impact without hitting a ceiling effect.

The Core Problem

The Context: Large language models can "teach themselves" (self-improvement) by generating answers, judging which ones are good, and training on those. This works because judging an answer is usually easier than generating one from scratch.

The Conflict: Two things are true in the current landscape:

  1. Small models are notoriously worse at judging their own answers than massive models.
  2. Deployed models are almost always quantized (shrunk to 8-bit or 4-bit) to run on-device.

The Research Question: Nobody appears to have studied what happens when you combine these two things. Does shrinking a model make its self-judgment worse, better, or unaffected? Can a quantized model reliably critique itself?

The Datasets

GSM8K (Grade School Math)

Type: Mathematical Reasoning (Open-ended).

Status: Our primary benchmark. It requires rigid logic and step-by-step reasoning, making it highly sensitive to extreme quantization. It provides a perfect "sweet spot" difficulty (~60% base accuracy for 1B models), allowing the verifier room to actually improve the outcome.

SVAMP & CQA

SVAMP Status: Dropped. Models hit a "ceiling effect" (~85% base accuracy), leaving almost no incorrect examples for the verifier to practice on.

CQA Status: De-prioritized. Multiple-choice tasks introduce "noise" where increased generation diversity just raises the chance of randomly guessing the correct letter, skewing the metrics.

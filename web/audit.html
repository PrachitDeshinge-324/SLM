1. The short version

  • Original question: does shrinking a small model (8-bit / 4-bit) break its ability to judge its own answers?
  • Answer so far: mostly no. 8-bit is indistinguishable from full precision. 4-bit costs accuracy for the weakest models, but the model's agreement signal ("my 8 samples agree, so I'm probably right") still works about as well as before.
  • Best finding: when 4-bit hurts accuracy, self-agreement drops by almost the same amount. That is a label-free damage detector.
  • What failed: the model-as-judge ("is this solution correct?") is weak for every model. It never beats a free rule: "approve if the answer matches the most common answer".
  • Not done: the self-improvement training loop (Experiment B) was never run, so nothing here says anything about it.

Numbers on this page are re-scored from the stored raw responses with the fixed scoring code. The older Phase 1 and Phase 2 pages show the pre-fix numbers.

Takeaways

  1. 8-bit is safe. Nothing differs from 16-bit beyond noise in any of the ten cells.
  2. 4-bit lowers accuracy by about 5 points on average (95% CI 3.8–6.6), in 8 of 10 cells, concentrated in the weakest models.
  3. Agreement still predicts correctness equally well at every precision (AUROC change under 0.01 on average). The "confidently wrong" outcome did not happen.
  4. Agreement drops when accuracy drops. Across 20 quantized runs the two drops correlate at r = 0.93 (0.79 without the three largest). Predicting a held-out model's accuracy change from its agreement change is off by 1.8 points, against 3.4 for "assume no change".
  5. Most answer flips are sampling noise. Two independent 4-sample votes from the same model disagree on 10–26% of questions. 16-bit vs 8-bit flips sit at that floor; at 4-bit only Gemma (+16 points) and MATH-500 (+5 to +7) clearly exceed it.
  6. Self-verification is weak. No model beats the free "approve if the answer equals the most common answer" rule. DeepSeek's judge is unusable (22–36% of outputs contain a verdict, almost all "Correct").
  7. One verifier lead: 4-bit judges approve more wrong answers (false-positive rate +0.155 Qwen-0.8B GSM8K, +0.144 Gemma-1B), with true-positive rate rising too, which looks like a shift toward "Correct" rather than lost skill. One seed, different trace pools: treat as a lead.

Where the evidence is weak

  • Six models, mostly GSM8K, one seed, 100 questions per cell.
  • The agreement–accuracy correlation leans on three large-drop cells.
  • MATH-500 at 4-bit has more than half its samples truncated.
  • No 3-bit and no non-bitsandbytes quantizer, so little real damage outside 4-bit on weak models.

5. Proposed contribution

For sampled reasoning, quantization damage shows up as a drop in the model's own self-agreement, roughly one-for-one with the accuracy drop. That lets you estimate how much accuracy a quantized model lost without any labels.

Why it may be new

Reliability papers use token-level uncertainty or greedy flips. Self-consistency papers stay at full precision. We found none that uses the shift in sampled agreement as a label-free damage estimator for quantization.

What we already have
  • Unchanged agreement-to-correctness AUROC
  • r = 0.93 between agreement drop and accuracy drop
  • Flip rates corrected against a sampling-noise floor
What would kill it

On new models or quantizers, predicting the accuracy change from the agreement change is no better than "assume no change", or the correlation falls below about 0.5.

6. What to run next (inference only)

  1. Rerun with the fixed prompts and a token limit large enough that truncation is below 5%. Run all three precisions of a model together.
  2. Add a second quantizer (GPTQ or GGUF Q4) and a 3-bit point, so there are more cells with real damage.
  3. Three seeds per cell, 100–200 questions.
  4. Compare the agreement-shift estimator against two label-free baselines: flip rate against the 16-bit model, and cross-model disagreement.
  5. Optional short negative result: freeze 16-bit traces and let each precision judge them, to settle the verifier question.

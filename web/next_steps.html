In one minute

We let a small language model (Qwen3.5-0.8B) teach itself grade-school math with no answer key. It answers each practice question several times, keeps the answers most of its attempts agree on, and trains on those. We ran the same loop on the full-precision model (16-bit) and on a compressed copy (4-bit).

The surprise

The same loop helped the compressed model and slightly hurt the full-precision one. The 4-bit model became right more often, mostly on the questions compression had broken. The 16-bit model only learned to finish its answers within the length limit, and its majority vote got worse.

This page explains what we think is happening and lists the experiments that could prove us wrong, in the order we plan to run them. It also says what a paper would need. Every number on it comes from one training run per precision on 200 test questions, so read them as a strong pilot, not a result. The full tables are on Phase 3: Labels & Self-Training.

This page replaces the earlier plan, which was written before the self-training loop had run.

Comparing the two directly (the 4-bit change minus the 16-bit change, question by question), the vote gains +11.0 points [+4.0, +18.5] more at 4-bit.

The 4-bit gain is mostly repair

Take the 37 test questions where, in separate earlier runs, the 16-bit model solved them at least 25 points more often than the 4-bit one: the questions compression broke. On those, the 4-bit model gains +14.5 points [+6.8, +22.6] and closes about half the gap. On the other questions it gains +4.3 [+0.8, +7.7].

The catch: the labels drift

At both precisions the model's own labels get worse each round. Label accuracy falls from 0.94 to 0.83 at 16-bit and from 0.92 to 0.81 at 4-bit by round 3. Labels added after training are only 57–81% right. The share of training examples with a wrong label rises from about 6% to 16–19%. At 4-bit, the gain on finished answers peaks at round 1 and then slowly erodes.

Why we think it happens

Training on your own majority answer does one thing: it makes the model more certain of the answer it already prefers. Researchers call this sharpening. Whether that helps depends on what kind of mistakes the model makes.

In short: a vote can remove noise, but it cannot fix a consistent error. It only makes that error more confident. Compression adds noise, which is why the compressed model benefits. This is our hypothesis. The experiments below are designed to test it.

Agreement rises faster than accuracy: 6–13 points against 1–6. Once a model has trained on its own agreement, rising agreement no longer shows that it improved.

Notes on the design choices
  • Random-answer control, done properly. Shuffling labels between questions would pair answers with the wrong questions and teach only format. Picking random finished answers to the same question keeps everything except the vote's choice, which is the one thing we want to remove.
  • 8-bit is not just "less noise". bitsandbytes 8-bit keeps its largest weights at 16 bits, and in our earlier runs 8-bit also differs from 16-bit by a floating-point format change. So we first confirm that its starting damage is in between (Phase 1: 0–2 vote points for this model, against about 7 at 4-bit) before reading it as a dose.
  • Model choice. We pick models whose damage we already measured in Phase 1, so they span the range. Gemma-3-1B is a special case: at 4-bit it mostly changed how it writes answers, so it tests a different kind of damage.
  • SVAMP. We set it aside earlier because larger models are near its ceiling. For 0.8–2B models there is smaller but real room: Qwen3.5-0.8B votes 83% right at 16-bit and loses about 4 points per answer at 4-bit. MATH-500 is likely too hard for 0.8B; keep it for the 2B model.
  • Fix the bookkeeping first. The report's loss column is wrong for runs under 20 steps, and the label statistics for preference (DPO) runs use the wrong threshold. Both must be fixed before replication. Training should also run a fixed number of passes over the data, not a fixed number of steps: today later rounds see each example less than once.

The pivot paper

Claim: a compressed model can recover part of the accuracy it lost by training on its own answers. No answer key and no full-precision model are needed, and no vote either.

Closest prior work: LLM-QAT (Liu et al., Findings of ACL 2024) trains a compressed model on text generated by the full-precision model. The pivot would use only the compressed model's own answers.

It would need: training on its own answers to other kinds of questions; the same training at 16-bit as a control; the 8-bit dose; and the borrowed teacher as the distillation reference.

The fallback paper

Claim: what a small compressed model actually learns from its own votes, and how to tell without an answer key. It rests on results that already hold, whatever Stage 1 shows:

  • Label drift, measured round by round in all four self-training runs so far, at both precisions.
  • The finishing trap: at 16-bit the whole gain is finishing within the length limit, and the split above tells it apart from real improvement.
  • A documented preference-training failure: the model drifted into a re-reading style found in none of its training examples.
  • From the Phase 1 data: the drop in self-agreement predicts the accuracy lost to compression on models it was not fitted to (0.85 points average error, against 3.36 for "assume no change"). The vote hides part of the damage. Compression leaves label accuracy unchanged in every model but one (Gemma-3-1B), and only reduces how many labels there are.

A workshop or Findings paper about measurement. Less striking than the precision story, but reusable.

How we will judge success

Fixed in advance. An experiment improves the model only if all five hold on 1,319 questions, at that precision:

  1. Single-answer accuracy up by at least 2 points, with an interval excluding zero.
  2. Vote accuracy no more than 1 point below the start.
  3. Accuracy of finished answers no more than 1 point below the start, so the gain is not just "finishes more".
  4. Coverage no more than 1.5 points below the start.
  5. Warning signals that training never optimises, such as the rate of arithmetic slips, move the same way as accuracy.

Label accuracy is tracked but is not a success criterion: the 4-bit run improved even while its labels drifted. How the current loop scores today (200 questions, so indicative only):

The revised training rule

If Stage 1 confirms that drift erodes the gain, the method we will test makes three changes to the loop:

answers again; keep finished

1. Frozen labels

Removes the drift loop by construction: a trained model never writes labels.

2. Evidence weights

Each question counts once, more or less depending on evidence the vote does not use: the model's own check of its answer, and re-computing the arithmetic in its working. A question backed only by the vote counts for less.

3. A tie set per precision

At 16-bit the tie keeps the variety of answers the vote depends on. At 4-bit it must stay weak: the untrained 4-bit model is the noisy one, and a strong tie would pull it back to its slips.

The rule as a formula
$$\mathcal L(\theta) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal C|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal C} w_q\,\overline{\ell}_\theta(S_q) \;+\; \lambda\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal P|}\sum_{q\in\mathcal P}\overline{\ell}_\theta(B_q)$$

$\overline{\ell}_\theta(Y)$ is the average per-token loss on a set of answers $Y$. $\mathcal C$ is the labelled questions; $S_q$ the current model's finished answers that match the frozen label of question $q$; $w_q \in [0, 1]$ its evidence weight. $\mathcal P$ is all practice questions; $B_q$ the untrained model's own answers to them. Answers cut off at the limit are included without an end token, so the tie keeps the original length distribution. The second term is a sampled estimate of how far the model has drifted from where it started. $\lambda$ is chosen separately per precision, without the answer key: the smallest value that keeps label-free warning signals within run-to-run noise.

For preference training (DPO), which made the model ramble in an earlier test, the plan is a bounded version (IPO-style, a small target margin) plus a term that keeps the preferred answer likely. In that test the model drifted into a "wait, let me re-read" style that appeared in none of its training examples.

Risks and cost

Compute

From our own runs on a T4 GPU: about 1.6 seconds per generated answer. One round at the current size (200 test and 500 practice questions, 8 answers each) takes about 3 hours.

  • Stage 1: about 30 GPU-hours. Frozen labels reuse the existing round 0.
  • Stage 2, per model: 3 seeds × 2 precisions at full size, about 170–210 GPU-hours.

A faster sampler (vLLM, tried once and reverted) is the biggest possible saving. Fewer test rounds (rounds 0, 1 and last only) is the next.

What could sink the story

  • The random-answer control gains as much: the vote is not the cause, and we switch to the pivot paper.
  • The effect does not replicate across seeds or models: we write the fallback paper.
  • Repair does not grow with damage: the noise explanation is wrong.
  • No label-free stopping rule works. Today we would not know without the answer key when to stop training, and that is an open problem in the field.

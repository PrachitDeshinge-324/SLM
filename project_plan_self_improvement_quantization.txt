================================================================================
PROJECT PLAN: DOES QUANTIZATION BREAK SELF-IMPROVEMENT IN SMALL
LANGUAGE MODELS?
================================================================================

A note on how to read this document: this is a scoping document, not a
finished research plan. It lays out the problem, the background needed to
understand it, what appears to already be known (based on literature search
carried out in September 2026), and what appears to still be open. Literature
moves fast in this area. Before committing serious time to any single
sub-question below, re-search it -- a paper may have appeared in the weeks
since this was written. Nothing here should be taken as a guarantee that a
gap still exists by the time you read it.

================================================================================
PART 1: THE PROBLEM, IN SIMPLE WORDS
================================================================================

Large language models can sometimes "teach themselves" -- get better at a
task by looking at their own attempts, deciding which ones seem good, and
training on those. This is called self-improvement. It works because models
are often better at judging an answer than at producing one in the first
place: it's easier to recognize a good essay than to write one from scratch.
Self-improvement tries to squeeze out that judging ability and turn it into
better answers.

Two things are true at the same time in the current research landscape:

1. Small models appear to be worse at judging their own answers than very
   large models. Several papers (listed below) have found this, though the
   picture is mixed rather than uniformly bad, and the exact point where
   this weakness shows up or goes away has not been mapped out carefully.

2. Models that are deployed for real use are almost never run at full
   precision. They are shrunk ("quantized") to save memory and run faster --
   this is standard practice for anything running on-device.

The open question this project explores is: NOBODY APPEARS TO HAVE STUDIED
WHAT HAPPENS WHEN YOU COMBINE THESE TWO THINGS. Does shrinking a model make
its self-judgment worse, better, or unaffected? And if a model is shrunk
and then asked to improve itself using only its own judgment (no outside
teacher, no ground-truth answers), does that process work, quietly fail, or
actively make the model worse?

This matters practically: if compressed, on-device models are increasingly
expected to personalize or improve themselves from usage (a real, emerging
deployment pattern), and if compression breaks the self-judgment this
depends on, that is a genuine and currently undocumented risk.

================================================================================
PART 2: BACKGROUND -- HOW SELF-IMPROVEMENT IN LANGUAGE MODELS WORKS
================================================================================

Below are the main mechanisms in roughly the order they appeared in the
literature. Read at least the first four before designing any experiment --
they define the vocabulary the rest of the field uses.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.1 STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Title:  "STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning"
Venue:  NeurIPS 2022
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14465

What it does, simply: show the model a question, have it write out its
reasoning and an answer. If the answer is correct, keep that reasoning as
training data. If it's wrong, give the model the correct answer and ask it
to explain backward how it could have gotten there ("rationalization"),
and use that explanation as training data too. Fine-tune on all of this,
repeat.

Model tested: GPT-J, 6B parameters. The paper explicitly notes GPT-2 (much
smaller) could NOT bootstrap even simple arithmetic reasoning this way --
an early, direct signal that this class of method needs a minimum amount
of base capability to get started.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.2 LMSI / "Large Language Models Can Self-Improve"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Title:  "Large Language Models Can Self-Improve"
Venue:  EMNLP 2023 (arXiv 2022)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11610

What it does, simply: instead of using the correct answer to check itself,
ask the model the same question many times with some randomness, and treat
the most common ("majority vote") answer as probably correct. Fine-tune on
the reasoning that led to that majority answer. No ground truth needed at
all.

Model tested: PaLM, 540 billion parameters. Also tested distilling the
self-improved 540B model's knowledge down to 8B and 62B models (a
different thing from those smaller models self-improving on their own).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.3 The Sharpening Mechanism (the main theory paper)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Title:  "Self-Improvement in Language Models: The Sharpening Mechanism"
Venue:  ICLR 2025 (arXiv 2412.01951)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01951

What it does, simply: this is the theoretical explanation for why
self-improvement can work at all, given that a model supposedly "can't
create information it doesn't already have." The answer: models are often
better at judging (verifying) a response than generating one, so
self-improvement works by using the model's own judgment to pick its best
outputs, then training on those picks -- "sharpening" the model toward
what it already secretly knows was good.

The math, in plain words:
  - "Coverage": a model can only be sharpened successfully if, somewhere
    in the wide range of things it could say, it does sometimes produce
    the right answer. If it never does, even by chance, there's nothing
    to sharpen.
  - They compare simple fine-tuning on your own best guesses (SFT) against
    reinforcement-learning-style training (RLHF). Plain fine-tuning is
    proven to be as good as any method can be, GIVEN enough coverage. RL-
    style training can do better than plain fine-tuning specifically
    because it can actively explore, which can work even when coverage is
    weak.

Models tested: Phi-3 (Mini/Small/Medium), Phi-3.5-Mini, Mistral-7B,
Llama-3.2-3B, GPT-3.5.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.4 "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Title:  "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet"
Venue:  ICLR 2024
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798

What it does, simply: tests whether asking a model to "double check your
answer" without giving it the correct answer actually helps. Finding: no
-- performance usually gets WORSE. Models flip more correct answers to
wrong than they fix wrong answers to correct, because they can't reliably
judge their own correctness without help. Also shows several earlier
"self-correction works" papers were secretly using the ground-truth answer
to know when to stop, which is a form of cheating.

Models tested: GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4-Turbo, Llama-2-70B.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
2.5 rStar-Math
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Title:  "rStar-Math: Small LLMs Can Master Math Reasoning with
        Self-Evolved Deep Thinking"
Venue:  arXiv 2501.04519 (2025)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04519

What it does, simply: shows small models (as small as 1.5B) CAN
self-improve strongly at math -- but only with heavy scaffolding: a
structured tree-search process (Monte Carlo Tree Search) plus a separate,
purpose-built model whose only job is scoring reasoning steps (a "Process
Reward Model"). The paper does not test whether the model can self-improve
using ONLY its own judgment, without this external scaffolding -- that
specific narrower question is not answered here.

Models tested: Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Phi3-mini-Instruct (3.8B),
Qwen2-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B.

================================================================================
PART 3: BACKGROUND -- HOW QUANTIZATION WORKS FOR LLMs, IN SIMPLE WORDS
================================================================================

A language model is, at its core, hundreds of millions or billions of
numbers (called weights). Normally these numbers are stored with high
precision (e.g., 16-bit floating point). Quantization means storing those
same numbers with less precision (e.g., 8-bit, 4-bit, or even fewer bits) --
similar to rounding 3.14159265 down to 3.14. This makes the model smaller
in memory and faster to run, at some cost to accuracy.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.1 When quantization happens in a model's lifecycle
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

(a) BEFORE serious training -- rare, only done by a few well-resourced
    labs: build the model to be "quantization-friendly" from the very
    first training step, so it survives shrinking better later
    ("quantization-aware pretraining").

(b) AFTER training, before deployment -- BY FAR the most common case:
    train the model normally at full precision, and once it's finished and
    working well, shrink it as a final step so it fits on a phone or runs
    cheaper on a server. This is called Post-Training Quantization (PTQ).
    Nothing about the training process changes; you are compressing a
    finished product. Common tools/methods: GPTQ, AWQ, bitsandbytes,
    HQQ, FP8.

(c) DURING a smaller follow-up training step -- fairly common in practice:
    take an already-shrunk model and do a bit more training on top of it
    (to specialize it, or to fix damage quantization caused), using a
    technique called QLoRA that lets you train without needing the full-
    size version in memory at the same time.

In one sentence: quantization almost always happens AFTER the main
training is finished, as a last step before deployment -- and only
sometimes is there a further, smaller round of training done on top of the
already-shrunk model afterward.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
3.2 What is already known about quantization's effects on LLM behavior
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

Quantization degrading reasoning accuracy is well established:

Title:  "Quantization Meets Reasoning: Exploring LLM Low-Bit Quantization
        Degradation for Mathematical Reasoning"
Venue:  arXiv 2501.03035 (2025)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03035
Finding: aggressive quantization (AWQ, GPTQ) causes up to 32% accuracy loss
on math reasoning for Llama-3 models. Also shows that fine-tuning the
quantized model on a small set of examples where the FULL-PRECISION
model's correct answers are used as an answer key can restore most of the
lost performance. This is supervised repair using an external reference
(the uncompressed model), not self-improvement.

Quantization degrading confidence/calibration is also established:

Title:  "Reliability Scaling Laws for Quantized Large Language Models"
Venue:  arXiv 2607.10855 (2026)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.10855
Finding: comprehensive reliability study (predictive uncertainty,
calibration, robustness to typos/perturbations) across 1B-70B models and
six quantization methods. Finds 4-bit is generally the best trade-off
point. This is about single-answer confidence, not about whether AGREEMENT
across multiple sampled answers still predicts correctness.

Quantization can quietly break things standard metrics don't catch (a
recurring pattern across several 2025-2026 papers):

Title:  "Quantization Undoes Alignment: Bias Emergence in Compressed LLMs
        Across Models and Precision Levels"
Venue:  arXiv 2605.15208 (2026), presented at IEEE Cloud Summit
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15208
Finding: 3-bit quantization causes 6-21% of previously-unbiased test items
to develop new stereotyped behavior, even though perplexity (the standard
quality metric) barely moves. The pattern this paper established --
"quantization looks harmless on the usual metric, but secretly breaks
something else nobody checked" -- has now been repeated for several other
properties (see below), but not yet for self-improvement / self-judgment.

Title:  "The Illusion of Equivalency: Statistical Characterization of
        Quantization Effects in LLMs"
Venue:  arXiv 2607.08734 (2026)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08734
Finding: introduces "Correctness Agreement" -- whether a quantized model
gets the same items right as the full-precision one. Finds behavioral
divergence even when overall accuracy looks unchanged. Studies attention-
weight structure, not self-judgment or self-consistency.

Title:  "Can Large Language Models Still Explain Themselves?
        Investigating the Impact of Quantization on Self-Explanations"
Venue:  arXiv 2601.00282 (2026)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00282
Finding: quantization causes moderate declines in the quality and
faithfulness of a model's explanations of its own reasoning. This is the
closest existing paper in SPIRIT to what this project proposes -- but it
studies EXPLANATION quality (can the model describe its reasoning
faithfully), not self-IMPROVEMENT (can the model use its own judgment to
get measurably better through training).

Quantization combined with test-time strategies (closest existing paper
to the framing in this project, IMPORTANT TO READ FIRST):

Title:  "Not All Bits Are Equal: Scale-Dependent Memory Optimization
        Strategies for Reasoning Models"
Venue:  ICLR 2026
Link:   (search title on arXiv / ICLR 2026 proceedings)
Finding: massive study (1,700+ configurations) of how to spend a fixed
memory budget across model size, weight precision (4/8/16-bit), how long
the model is allowed to "think," and how many times you sample-and-vote
(majority voting / self-consistency), across Qwen3 (0.6B to 32B),
DeepSeek-R1-Distill, and OpenReasoning-Nemotron.
KEY FINDING: below a certain effective size (their threshold: roughly
"8-bit-4B"), sampling multiple answers and voting is NOT memory-efficient
-- you get more accuracy per gigabyte by using a bigger/less-shrunk model
instead of voting. Above that threshold, voting starts to pay off, and the
optimal number of votes increases with available memory.
IMPORTANT CAVEAT: this paper explicitly limits itself to INFERENCE ONLY --
no fine-tuning of any kind is performed. It also does not check whether,
when the model's sampled answers agree, that agreement still means the
answer is actually correct -- it only checks whether voting is worth the
memory cost for improving raw accuracy. Those are two different questions.
This paper explicitly lists quantization-aware training and other
training-time approaches as future work, not something it covers.

Title:  "QuantLRM: Quantization of Large Reasoning Models via Fine-Tuning
        Signals"
Venue:  arXiv 2602.02581 (2026)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02581
Finding: uses signals FROM a fine-tuning process (SFT/DPO/RL) to decide
which parts of the model to protect (keep at higher precision) when
quantizing. This is a quantization TECHNIQUE informed by training, not
self-improvement running on top of an already-quantized model. Opposite
direction from what this project explores.

================================================================================
PART 4: SELF-JUDGMENT RELIABILITY IN SMALL/MID-SIZED MODELS (full
        precision) -- WHAT'S ALREADY BEEN MEASURED
================================================================================

These papers establish that model self-judgment reliability is a real,
already-documented concern at full precision, and that it does not appear
to be a simple, fully solved question at any size tested so far. This
project asks whether quantization makes it better, worse, or unaffected;
these papers are the full-precision baseline you would be extending.

Title:  "When Should a Language Model Trust Itself? Same-Model
        Self-Verification as a Conditional Confidence Signal"
Venue:  arXiv 2605.02915 (2026)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02915
Finding: tests self-verification (does the model's own judgment of
correctness track actual correctness) across TinyLlama-1.1B, Qwen-1.5B,
Phi-2 (2.7B), Qwen-7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-8B. Result: "clear but narrow"
-- works well on some benchmarks (ARC-Challenge), much less reliably on
others (TruthfulQA-MC). EXPLICITLY FLAGS AS OPEN QUESTION (their own
wording, paraphrased): whether reasoning-native models escape this
unreliability is not tested and is left open.

Title:  "More Yap Less Meaning: Uncovering Self-Improvement Behavior in
        SLMs"
Venue:  arXiv 2606.08471 (2026)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08471
Finding: even when GIVEN the correct answer and asked to explain what went
wrong, small-to-mid models (1.5B-8B, including Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B,
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, Gemma-2-2B, Llama-3.1-8B, Phi-4-mini)
improve only 4.4% on average. Longer self-generated explanations correlate
with WORSE outcomes, not better.

Title:  "When Self-Consistency Backfires: Majority Vote Hurts the Majority
        of Hard Science Problems for Small LLMs"
Venue:  arXiv 2608.11403 (2026)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11403
Finding: on hard science questions (GPQA Diamond), majority voting --
the core mechanism LMSI (section 2.2 above) relies on -- REDUCES accuracy
on the majority of problems for Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3-8B, at full
precision. Confidence (how much samples agree) does not reliably track
correctness on hard problems. EXPLICITLY FLAGS AS OPEN QUESTION (their own
wording, paraphrased): "whether reasoning-native models escape this is the
key open question" -- they deliberately did not test reasoning-specialized
models, and note this as the natural next step for someone else.

Title:  "Do Self-Evolving Agents Forget? Capability Degradation and
        Preservation in Lifelong LLM Agent Adaptation"
Venue:  arXiv 2605.09315 (2026)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09315
Finding: self-evolving agents lose old capabilities as they gain new ones
("capability erosion"), consistently across workflow, skill, model, and
memory-based self-evolution. For the MODEL self-evolution dimension
specifically (STaR-style fine-tuning), tested on Qwen3-0.6B and
Llama3.2-3B. Proposes a fix (Capability-Preserving Evolution, based on an
older anti-forgetting technique called Elastic Weight Consolidation) that
improves retention on both sizes tested. Does not test quantization at
all.

Title:  "On the Generalization Gap in Self-Evolving Language Model
        Reasoning"
Venue:  arXiv 2606.01075 (2026)
Link:   https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.01075
Finding: whether models in the 1.5B-4B range can self-evolve successfully
is INCONSISTENT across different research groups' results -- some find it
works fine, others find it doesn't. EXPLICITLY STATES (own wording,
paraphrased): "this suggests self-improvement may be highly sensitive to
the domain or model family" -- treats this inconsistency itself as an
open, unresolved question rather than claiming a clean answer either way.

================================================================================
PART 5: THE PROPOSED EXPERIMENTS
================================================================================

These are ordered by cost and by how directly they test the gap. Model
size in both experiments below should be chosen based on what is actually
available and runnable on the student's hardware -- there is no reason to
restrict this project to any particular narrow parameter range. Reasonable
candidates span roughly 0.5B up to 8B (e.g., Qwen3 0.6B/1.7B/4B/8B,
Llama-3.2 1B/3B alongside Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-Math or DeepSeek-R1-
Distill-Qwen at 1.5B/7B). Testing across several sizes within whatever
range is feasible is more informative than testing a single size, since it
lets you look for a trend rather than one data point -- but the specific
sizes chosen should be driven by hardware availability, not by an assumed
boundary in the literature.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
EXPERIMENT D (start here): Does compression break the reliability of
self-judgment, with NO training involved?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question: as you shrink a model from full precision down through 8-bit and
4-bit, does "how often its sampled answers agree with each other" still
mean "the answer is actually correct" -- or does the model become falsely
confident (agreeing a lot, but wrongly) under compression?

Why start here: this is inference-only. No fine-tuning is needed. It is
the cheapest possible test of the core idea and gives a fast go/no-go
signal before investing in training experiments.

Suggested setup:
  - Models: pick 2-4 sizes from whatever is available and runnable, ideally
    spanning as wide a range as your hardware allows (see note above).
  - Quantization: GPTQ or bitsandbytes 8-bit/4-bit are the most standard,
    well-documented, easy-to-set-up choices. AWQ is a reasonable
    alternative; the "Not All Bits Are Equal" paper found GPTQ, AWQ, and
    FP8 give very similar accuracy-memory curves, so the specific method
    likely matters less than the precision level.
  - Precision levels to test: at minimum, full precision (16-bit), 8-bit,
    4-bit. Add 3-bit only if the model still functions at all at that
    level (see "floor effect" note in Part 6).
  - Task/benchmark: GSM8K or a subset of MATH for math reasoning (widely
    used, easy to auto-grade); ARC-Challenge or CommonsenseQA if you want
    a non-math angle.
  - Procedure: for each (model, precision) pair, sample N answers per
    question (N=8 to 16 is a reasonable range used elsewhere in this
    literature), compute the majority-vote answer and its "confidence"
    (fraction of samples agreeing), and separately record whether that
    majority answer is actually correct. Plot: does the relationship
    between confidence and correctness change as precision drops?
  - This closely follows the calibration/reliability methodology used in
    "Reliability Scaling Laws for Quantized LLMs" (Part 3.2) and the
    self-verification methodology in "When Should a Language Model Trust
    Itself?" (Part 4) -- both can be used as methodological templates.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
EXPERIMENT B (the main event): Self-improve a model that starts already
quantized
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Question: if you start from an already-shrunk model (the realistic
deployment scenario -- an on-device model only ever exists in its
compressed form) and run an actual self-improvement loop -- generate
answers, keep/train on the ones the model judges as good (via majority
vote or self-reward, no ground truth, no external teacher) -- does the
model actually improve, stay flat, or get worse?

Why this is the most useful of the two: this matches how self-improving
on-device models would really be used. There is no full-precision copy
available in that scenario, so this is the scenario that matters most for
real deployment risk.

Suggested setup:
  - Take a model, quantize it (as in Experiment D).
  - Fine-tune the quantized model using a parameter-efficient method that
    is compatible with quantized weights (QLoRA is the standard, widely-
    used choice for this -- training on top of a quantized base without
    needing the full-precision weights in memory).
  - Self-improvement loop options (pick one to start, based on what's
    feasible in your time/compute budget):
      (i) LMSI-style: generate multiple samples, keep the ones matching
          the majority-vote answer, fine-tune on those, repeat.
      (ii) STaR-style: generate reasoning + answer; for wrong answers,
           optionally regenerate with the correct answer as a hint
           (only if you have ground-truth labels for training questions,
           e.g. GSM8K's training set answers -- this is standard in the
           STaR paper and not "cheating" for training purposes, since
           STaR uses training-set ground truth, only test-time evaluation
           is unsupervised).
  - Compare the resulting model's accuracy after 1-3 iterations against
    the quantized model with no self-improvement applied (baseline).
  - Run across the same 2-4 sizes used in Experiment D so you can look for
    a size-dependent trend in whether self-improvement helps, does
    nothing, or hurts once the model starts out quantized.

================================================================================
PART 6: POSSIBLE OUTCOMES AND HOW TO PIVOT
================================================================================

Below are outcome scenarios specifically for Experiment D (the cheapest,
first experiment), since it determines what to do next.

Outcome 1 -- Clean breakdown: agreement/confidence stays high while actual
correctness drops as the model is shrunk.
  -> This is the strongest possible result for this project. Proceed
  directly to Experiment B, using this calibration gap as the explanation
  for whatever you find there.

Outcome 2 -- No real change: self-judgment reliability looks about the
same at every precision level tested.
  -> Report this honestly; it corrects an intuitive assumption and is
  still a valid, reportable finding. IMPORTANT: do not treat this as a
  reason to skip Experiment B. A self-improvement loop runs many rounds,
  and a small, invisible-at-single-measurement bias can compound after
  several rounds of training a model on its own outputs (this compounding
  failure mode is documented, without quantization, in "Progress or
  Regress? Self-Improvement Reversal in Post-training," ICLR 2025,
  arXiv 2407.05013, and in "Self-Improvement Can Self-Regress: The Rise-
  and-Collapse Failure Mode of LLM Self-Training," arXiv 2606.21090).
  Proceed to B, but treat it as more exploratory since you no longer have
  an early warning sign predicting the outcome.

Outcome 3 -- Non-monotonic: reliability holds up or even improves at
moderate compression (e.g. 8-bit), then collapses sharply at aggressive
compression (4-bit or below).
  -> This is the most scientifically interesting outcome, and matches a
  pattern seen in other quantization papers (moderate compression can
  sometimes sharpen a model's output distribution in a way that briefly
  looks like more confidence). Pin down exactly where the cliff occurs,
  and focus Experiment B narrowly on the precision levels just before and
  after that cliff, to test whether the training loop breaks at the same
  point.

Outcome 4 -- Messy / inconsistent across models or tasks, no single clean
trend.
  -> Also not a dead end. Several papers in this space (e.g. "On the
  Generalization Gap in Self-Evolving Language Model Reasoning," Part 4)
  made exactly this kind of inconsistency itself the main finding, rather
  than forcing a false clean narrative. If this happens, pick the 1-2
  model/precision combinations with the clearest effect (in either
  direction) as focused case studies for Experiment B, rather than trying
  to claim a universal rule.

Outcome 5 -- Floor effect: at aggressive quantization (e.g. 3-bit or
below) the model's task performance collapses so completely that there is
nothing meaningful left to measure.
  -> This is a methods issue, not a scientific dead end. Simply restrict
  the comparison to the precision range where the model still functions
  well enough to produce meaningful answers (commonly 16-bit vs 8-bit vs
  moderate 4-bit) -- this range is also the one actually used in most
  real deployments, so it does not weaken the practical relevance of the
  work.

General note: quantization is a secondary axis layered on top of a
question (does self-judgment/self-improvement break down at any given
model scale) that is independently real and already partially open
regardless of quantization. If quantization turns out to add nothing
interesting on top of that core question, the fallback is simply to
report the model-size finding on its own and drop the quantization angle
-- not to abandon the project.

================================================================================
PART 7: RISK ASSESSMENT
================================================================================

Overall field crowdedness: HIGH. Self-improvement, self-verification, and
quantization are each, on their own, extremely active research areas in
2025-2026, with multiple dedicated surveys and dozens of papers per year.
A student should expect to be competing for attention within a busy
literature, even if the specific intersection studied here is not yet
covered.

Crowdedness of the specific intersection (quantization x self-improvement
training): appears LOW based on three separate rounds of literature search
in September 2026 using varied search terms. No paper was found that runs
an actual self-improvement training loop (generate -> self-judge ->
fine-tune -> repeat, without an external teacher or ground-truth oracle)
on a quantized model. This should be treated as "not found," not as
"proven not to exist" -- it is possible a very recent or differently-
worded paper was missed, and the field is moving fast enough that this
could change within weeks. RE-CHECK THIS BEFORE COMMITTING SIGNIFICANT
TIME, and again before submitting any resulting paper.

Specific risks:
  - Being scooped: real, given how active the adjacent literatures are.
    Mitigation: do the cheap Experiment D first; it can be run quickly
    enough to get an early result even if a competing paper appears.
  - Model/compute availability: Experiment D requires only inference and
    is runnable on modest hardware for models up to several billion
    parameters once quantized. Experiment B requires fine-tuning, which is
    more demanding, but QLoRA-style training on a quantized model is
    specifically designed to be lightweight -- this is one of the reasons
    it is a realistic choice for a student project.
  - Messy/negative results: addressed directly in Part 6 above; every
    outcome has a documented next step.
  - Reviewer skepticism that a "diagnostic" paper (measuring a problem)
    without a proposed fix is insufficient for a strong venue: worth
    keeping in mind when deciding how to frame results, and worth
    discussing with an advisor once initial results from Experiment D/B
    are in hand.

================================================================================
PART 8: SUGGESTED PRACTICAL NOTES
================================================================================

- Compute: Experiment D can plausibly be run with modest cloud GPU credits
  or a single high-end consumer GPU, since it is inference-only across a
  handful of quantized models. Experiment B needs GPU time for QLoRA-style
  fine-tuning; scope the number of self-improvement iterations (start with
  1-3 rounds, not the 20-40 rounds sometimes used in the original STaR
  paper) to keep this tractable.
- Tools: standard, well-documented open-source options exist for every
  step here -- Hugging Face Transformers for models, GPTQ/AWQ/bitsandbytes
  libraries for quantization, and standard PEFT/QLoRA libraries for
  fine-tuning on quantized weights. None of this requires building new
  infrastructure from scratch.
- Suggested order of work: (1) re-verify the literature gap is still open,
  (2) run Experiment D across a few model sizes and 3 precision levels on
  one benchmark, (3) based on the outcome (see Part 6), decide how to
  proceed to Experiment B, (4) run Experiment B and compare against the
  no-self-improvement quantized baseline.
